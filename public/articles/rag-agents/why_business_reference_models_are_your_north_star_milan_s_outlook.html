From Chaos to Clarity

The business architecture and reference model are useful for corporate organizations to handle and address organizational changes. This method provides a systematic means of imagining a perfect future condition while closely monitoring advantages, expenses, and possible hazards. Nonetheless, establishing a coherent and consistent vision of the organization’s current and desired future is one of its most significant challenges.

In this regard, the Business Reference Model (BRM) is a revolutionary paradigm that revolutionizes how public and commercial organizations approach change. Consider the BRM a common language for explaining the objectives and structure of an organization. It enables even the most complicated companies to get everyone on the same page, much like a high-resolution map.

By adopting the BRM, leaders can:

  • Cut through the fog of organizational complexity
  • Align teams around a common vision
  • Identify the most impactful areas for improvement
  • Design change initiatives with precision
  • Track progress more effectively

The Business Reference Model (BRM) was created with the following ideas in mind.

  • Generic in the sense that it applies to organizations of all sizes and in any industry, from small and agile entrepreneurial start-ups to giant corporate companies with various lines of business.
  • Extendable beyond corporate borders to encompass suppliers, partners, and customers.
  • Customizable to appropriately depict the business functions of an organization.

The BRM is designed to facilitate the description of a business model from five perspectives:

  • Environmental : The Environmental perspective focuses on the circumstances in which an organization must operate. It describes the company’s external elements, such as competitors, regulations, and customers, as well as its overall market positioning strategy. This point of view is designed to explain why an organization is motivated to pursue specific courses of action.
  • Value : The Value Proposition perspective describes an organization’s product, service, brand, and shareholder value. This perspective is designed to describe an organization’s projected impact and how it will provide value for stakeholders.
  • Operating Model: The Operating Model perspective specifies the resources that the business will use to generate the value proposition. This perspective aims to describe how an organization will be able to deliver on its value proposition. Capabilities can be defined as a collection of people, processes, information, and technology that can be sourced internally or externally.
  • Risk : The risk perspective identifies the uncertainties that may exist in a company’s delivery of the value promise. This perspective is meant to describe the threats that an organization faces both inside and externally.
  • Compliance: The Compliance perspective represents a set of standards that the organization must follow to ensure that the value offer is delivered in accordance with approved business practices. This approach aims to highlight the limits that prevent a company from acting in harmful, damaging, or inappropriate ways, as well as the opportunities that can be capitalized on from a differentiated compliance position.

Factors within “Environment perspective”

Market Context

  • Examines overall industry value chain
  • Considers supplier-provider relationships
  • Looks at regulatory constraints, tax regimes, international accords
  • Analyzes market value, cost and value distribution
  • Considers industry trends and relationships with adjacent industries

Competitors

  • Examines organizations with similar capabilities
  • Focuses on understanding competitors’ value propositions
  • Measures comparative quality and performance attributes (customer satisfaction, retention, market share, profitability)

Customers

  • Seeks to understand current and potential customers
  • Provides an “outside-in” perspective
  • Analyzes market potential (customer base size and consumption level)
  • Segments customers (types and needs differences)
  • Examines customer journey and experience
  • Evaluates customer satisfaction (needs fulfillment and response)
  • Culture and values influence product vs. customer orientation

Strategy

  • Establishes organizational drivers and objectives
  • Shapes value proposition and operating model
  • Assumes strategic statements as “givens” for assessment
  • Makes explicit choices about ambition level and focus areas
  • Drives capability development and prioritization

Factors within “Value perspective”

Product

  • Tangible asset produced by a company for customers.
  • Satisfies business needs or solves business problems.
  • Standardized and reproducible quality.
  • Considerations: product portfolio analysis, pricing, features, marketing messages, customer segments, sales volumes, customer satisfaction, customer retention, future development roadmaps.

Service

  • Production of tangible benefit via a process.
  • Satisfies identified business needs through the exchange.
  • Quality varies depending on the service provider.
  • Core offering for service-based organizations to generate revenue.
  • Supporting services like “customer care” for customer interaction. Applies to all organizations, not always resulting in financial exchange.
  • Examples: government agencies providing public services.
  • Considerations: processes, service levels, user journeys and stories, cost-to-serve models.

Brand

  • Name, term, design, or symbol identifying a seller’s product from another.
  • Form's identity associated with business, product, or service.
  • Customer or shareholder forms emotional attachment.
  • Modelled through design, experience, and values.
  • Aligns with positive perceptions to ensure clear and effective value proposition.

Shareholder Value

  • Value obtained by shareholders through financial investment in a company.
  • Primary goal: increase shareholder value.
  • Composite of operating profit, assets, and future prospects perception.
  • Value components mix varies by organization.
  • Critical to understand what shareholders value before making changes.

Factors within “Operating Model perspective”

Value Chain

  • Examines the role of the business within the broader industry value chain.
  • Models the flows from source suppliers to the enterprise and from the enterprise to ultimate consumers.
  • Focuses on optimizing organizational positioning by analyzing the supply chain and market channels.

Capabilities

  • Modular units combining people, process, technology, and information to execute organizational functions.
  • Includes modeling and analyzing endtoend processes (e.g., order to cash) to understand dependencies and handover points between silos.

Governance

  • Executive functions consume management information to steer business direction.
  • Focuses on providing models for reporting, analytics, performance management, quality management, financial governance, product portfolio management, and communication.

Partners & Ecosystem

  • Examines internal and external entities supporting the business ecosystem.
  • Focuses on generating competitive advantage through partnerships and defining capabilities to be sourced from partners.
  • Analyzes levels of partnership, partnering methods, and preferred partners for various purposes.

Finance

  • Examines the usage of financial instruments for business operations and change implementation.
  • Models' revenue streams and cost profiles, and supports analysis such as fixed vs. variable costs, capital vs. revenue, budgeting, and cash flow.
  • Focuses on the flows of money through the organization, not on the corporate capabilities required for the Finance function.

Assets

  • Examines objects owned by the business that support operations (e.g., IT systems, office buildings, machinery).
  • Asset modeling is specific to asset class or business industry.

Factors within “Risk”

Financial

  • Potential of loss arising from an entity not meeting its financial obligations.

Operational

  • Internal or external factors impacting the entity’s ability to manage business operations or service customers.

Strategic

  • Potential impact to an entity’s value proposition or operating model due to external market factors.

Controls

  • Risk that the compliance regime is not followed or is insufficient to ensure compliant behavior.

Factors within “Compliance”

Commercial

  • Supplier interaction, contracting, and procurement
  • Consistent practices and standards of behavior
  • Policies and controls for bribery and corruption risks
  • Ensure effective use of funding
  • Mitigate credit and counterparty risks Operational
  • Internal or external factors impacting the entity’s ability to manage business operations or service customers.

Quality

  • Processes and controls for product and service quality
  • Alignment with value proposition
  • Compliance with other factors

Legal and Regulatory

  • Subject to various laws and regulations
  • Understanding constraints for large businesses
  • Identifying significant constraints
  • Support risk-based decision-making

Safety

  • Education and training to prevent injuries
  • Actions for inspections and audits
  • Post injury investigations support by health and safety bodies

Social Responsibility

  • Environmental initiatives for compliance
  • Forms include reporting and carbon trading
