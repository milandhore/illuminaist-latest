Architecting Tomorrow’s Data Lakes: Modernization Roadmaps and Engineering Excellence

T he concept of a “data lake” — a vast digital reservoir for information storage — often sparks diverse interpretations and responses among professionals in the data analytics realm. This metaphorical body of data can mean different things to different experts, each viewing it through their unique professional lens and experience. Data lakes have come a long way since their inception, evolving from a concept that once sparked skepticism to a powerful tool that’s revolutionizing data analytics. Gone are the days when data lakes were plagued by limitations and inefficiencies.

Today’s modern data lakes have addressed many of the challenges that hindered their predecessors, offering a robust solution for organizations of all sizes and across various industries. The transformation of data lakes has been nothing short of remarkable. They now boast features that were once thought impossible, such as seamless data modification, high-performance analytics, and enhanced data quality and security measures. This evolution has turned data lakes into versatile repositories capable of meeting diverse analytical needs without vendor lock-in — a dream come true for many organizations.

Imagine having a vast, fully managed storage area at your fingertips, ready to accommodate an ever-growing volume of data. It’s an enticing prospect, but the true magic lies in harnessing this data effectively. To unlock the full potential of a data lake, organizations must embark on a journey of data refinement. This process involves defining, transforming, cataloging, quality-checking, and structuring the data to make it accessible and valuable for both technical and non-technical users alike.

Why Use a Modern Data Lake?

In the era of big data, a cutting-edge data lake offers the robust capabilities of a data warehouse without the limitations of outdated Hadoop-based systems. These contemporary data lakes embrace an open architecture, liberating organizations from the shackles of vendor-specific solutions and technological dead-ends when designing, constructing, and tapping into their vast data reservoirs. Perhaps most crucially, businesses harnessing the power of modern data lakes retain full ownership and control over their invaluable information assets.

In the wake of Hadoop’s arrival, organizations embraced this groundbreaking technology to amass vast troves of diverse data from myriad origins. This raw, unprocessed information became a playground for data scientists, who delved into its depths to extract insights. However, business intelligence experts, armed with conventional SQL and reporting tools, found themselves grappling with the chaotic nature of this data lake, struggling to unearth its true value. The sluggish performance stood in stark contrast to the swift efficiency of traditional data warehouses. Only after the data underwent rigorous processing did it find its way, through replication, into the structured confines of a data warehouse.

Building a Modern Data Lake

Ingestion and landing of data

Embarking on the journey of modern data lake construction, we encounter extraction — the crucial first step in the ETL (extract, transform, load) process. This initial phase serves as the foundation for harnessing the power of disparate data sources. A plethora of cutting-edge tools and innovative techniques await data professionals, offering myriad ways to unlock valuable information from diverse storage systems.

When it comes to liberating data from its original confines, two main approaches reign supreme: the dynamic “push” method and the proactive “pull” strategy. These extraction techniques form the backbone of efficient data retrieval, enabling organizations to tap into their vast reservoirs of information with precision and finesse.

Pull is the traditional method of extracting data from source systems such as databases or NoSQL systems. It involves running SQL queries against the source system to extract new or modified data and insert it into the data lake
Push has become more popular based on data being produced at a rate never seen before. Data from sensors or cable TV boxes (for example) is often “pushed” into a data lake using various tools and methods, such as traditional secure file transfers and real-time APIs. Pushed data arrives in the lake in raw form.

With the explosion of real-time data sources, newer technologies such as Kafka, an open-source distributed event streaming platform, was designed to handle massive amounts of data while maintaining reliability and acting as a company’s “data queue” to populate a data lake. You can employ strategies for both “batch” and “streaming” ingestion — — —

Batch . Data is pulled or pushed into a data lake at given intervals.
Streaming (or micro-batch). Data is pushed into the data lake in real-time or near real-time

Data that arrives in the landing area of a data lake is either new data or an alteration to data that already exists in the lake, which is considered an update.Data lake (and data warehouse) data is typically not deleted. Data can provide an audit trail from many different source systems. For example, if a customer’s address changes, the original address would remain in the lake to ensure queries and reports could accurately capture historical data. This is known as a slowly changing dimension (SCD). Table formats such as Iceberg, Hudi, and Delta Lake enable you to merge and update data within a modern data lake

Structuring and transforming data

In the realm of data lakes, the journey of information doesn’t end at ingestion. Like a master chef refining raw ingredients, data undergoes a metamorphosis through transformation and enrichment processes. This culinary analogy extends as disparate data streams are artfully combined with existing datasets, much like blending complementary flavors. The result? A rich tapestry of insights, seasoned with business context, product details, and financial nuances.

Enter the era of modern data lakes, where cutting-edge table formats like Apache Iceberg and Delta Lake reign supreme. These innovative structures empower data lakes to rival traditional databases in functionality. Picture a digital ecosystem where data isn’t just stored, but lives and breathes, ready to be shaped and reshaped at will. This dynamic environment blurs the lines between storage and computation, ushering in a new age of data agility and insight generation.

Aggregation and rollups

Imagine your company’s sales data as a vast ocean of information. It’s incredibly detailed, but sometimes too intricate for quick analysis. Data captured from source systems is often too granular for ad hoc querying and reporting.

Product sales are good examples. The questions asked of this data are usually focused on sales during different time frames such as daily, weekly, monthly, and yearly. In order to provide faster performance when these types of queries are executed, data is usually pre-aggregated into different levels of granularity. This is often called “rolling up” the data into different structures to aid in reporting queries and are most commonly used by standard reporting tools such as Power BI and Tableau.

Governance and security

You need to implement proper security methods to ensure users accessing the data are correctly authenticated and authorized. This is essential to avoid all users gaining unfettered access and turning your data lake into a data swamp. You can use two main forms of security within a modern data lake: role-based access control ( RBAC ) and attribute-based access control ( ABAC )

RBAC grants privileges to roles that users are assigned. This is the standard method for the majority of relational and non-relational databases.
ABAC is a newer method of securing assets within a modern data lake. It uses tags to provide or deny privileges. Objects within the lake are tagged, and privileges are granted to the tag. Any object with that tag applied to it adheres to those privileges. This is a very powerful security mechanism because tags can span many different resource types.

Additionally, modern data lakes should have detailed audit logging as a mandatory requirement for every service and operation within the lake.

Cataloging and Data Products

Data lakes offer immense scalability, capable of storing vast amounts of data. The ability to locate and trust this data is crucial for the success of any data lake. Historically, data was added to lakes with minimal attention to structure and documentation, with the intention of cleansing and processing it later. This approach often resulted in data swamps.

Modern data lakes, however, have adopted a more disciplined methodology. Today, any data ingested into the lake must adhere to a defined structure. A centralized catalog is employed to track and document these structures.

Cataloging

As data is ingested into the lake, it must be cataloged. Adding metadata enhances the ability of end users to search for and locate relevant data sets. Data assets can be tagged (as detailed in the “Governance and Security” section) to enforce security rules and categorize these assets, thereby simplifying access.

Data Products: Unlocking the True Value of Your Organization’s Data

In today’s data-driven world, data is arguably your organization’s most valuable asset. However, finding trustworthy and curated data sets remains a significant challenge for many companies. This challenge has led to the development of data products.

The concept of data products emerged as a fundamental component of the increasingly popular data mesh architecture. A data mesh is a decentralized data management approach where ownership and control reside with individual domain teams. This promotes scalability, agility, and better alignment with business goals.

Traditionally, business intelligence (BI) tools provided a centralized area where users could find and maintain reports and structures. As more tools became available, both SQL and NoSQL-based, users often had to replicate the same structures across different BI tools.

Data products are curated data sets designed to address specific, targeted business questions. Users can access a searchable list of data products, complete with metadata such as tags, owners, trustworthiness, and age. This allows users to query data products using a variety of tools, protecting them from vendor lock-in.

Data products have been compared to a semantic layer, which adds context to data for business users. However, data products go beyond semantic layers by incorporating features such as in-depth metadata, lineage, approval processes, contracts, and social elements like shareability.
