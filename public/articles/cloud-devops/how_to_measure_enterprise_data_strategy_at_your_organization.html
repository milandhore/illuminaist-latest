Data Usage Maturity Stages

The first step in building a data strategy is to understand where you are in terms of data usage maturity. The following diagram presents different stages in data usage maturity, from transactional (stage 1) to data-driven (stage 4).

Stage 1 (Transactional)

In this stage, companies are focused on their core operations. They don’t take advantage of the data around those operations, because they don’t measure or use financial and operational performance indicators for their business. Today, we see very few companies at this stage. Most of these are startup companies in the early stages of their business.

Stage 2 (Informed by data)

In this Stage, companies use data to monitor their business health in terms of operational, financial, and departmental data that is analyzed inside each department in a siloed manner. Most enterprises that are at this stage have on-premises, proprietary systems, where sharing the data can be complex and expensive.

Moving from stage 2 companies to the cloud usually involves enabling them to extract, catalog, and share data between business areas, and start using advanced interactive analysis.

Stage 3 (Based on data)

Stage 3 includes companies that have already optimized their data usage. These companies use their data in different ways, depending on the industry. Service companies such as financial services, healthcare services, e-commerce services, and consumer packaged goods services know their customers’ behaviors. They use data to create timely recommendations and offers based on these behaviors.

Financial, Insurance, or Manufacturing companies often use advanced forecasting analysis to optimize their production and supply operations.

Agricultural and manufacturing companies use data to optimize their logistics operations, improve process efficiency, and implement precision agriculture. However, although the companies in stage 3 use data extensively, they require manual data analysis to take these actions.

Most companies today are in stage 3, although some of them use more advanced techniques such as machine learning (ML) models, and some are starting to experiment with advanced analytics.

Stage 4 (Driven by data)

Companies in stage 4 are already making decisions, often automatically, based on their data. However, this can be challenging. It requires confidence in the data and mechanisms in place for applications to use and react to the data. Stage 4 also requires data to be available for timely decision-making.

Reversible ( two-way door) decisions are great candidates for data-driven actions. For example, a company might decide to quarantine a product (stop selling it) after it receives negative reviews that represent a statistically high probability of product returns or customer complaints. The quarantine is reversible after the issue has been addressed, and the product can be placed back on sale.

Fraud detection is another example of a two-way, data-driven action. Companies might introduce mechanisms to avoid loss for their customers and platform, even if they encounter some false positives that have to be addressed. They can introduce improvements by measuring the results of current mechanisms and evaluating their effectiveness. After false positives are mitigated or validated by customers, transactions can be confirmed or retried by using two-factor authentication or a similar process.

However, some actions aren’t easily reversible and require further discussion and approval by a board of executives. These are called one-way door decisions. For example, actions that involve the construction of facilities or significant money investments are usually hard to reverse. These aren’t good candidates for automatic data-driven actions.

A data-driven action should be evaluated for the visibility of its impact through constant measurement. These measurements help you decide to roll back a feature or to test and engage a team for a deeper analysis of distinct behavior.

Data Governance and Automation

Data governance is a key enabler for data strategy. Data governance refers to the processes that ensure that data is standardized, classified, and quality-controlled across the organization1. Data governance also facilitates data sharing and discovery by providing metadata, lineage, cataloging, security, and access control mechanisms.

Data governance should not be seen as a burden or an overhead for data teams. Rather, it should be seen as an opportunity to automate manual tasks, reduce errors, increase productivity, and enhance collaboration.

Collaborate your strategy and Stage 4 operationalization using the Public Cloud provides. For example, let’s consider offerings from AWS.

AWS provides various tools and services that support data governance, such as AWS Glue, AWS Lake Formation, AWS Data Exchange, Amazon Athena, Amazon Redshift, Amazon EMR, Amazon S3, and AWS IAM. These tools and services help you to

  • Ingest data from various sources and formats, such as databases, files, streams, APIs, and third-party providers.
  • Transform and enrich data using code or visual interfaces, such as AWS Glue Data Brew
  • Catalog and classify data using schemas, tags, and crawlers.
  • Enforce data quality rules and validations using AWS Glue Data Quality
  • Secure and audit data access using policies, roles, encryption, and logging.
  • Discover and explore data using search, query, and visualization tools.

Data Strategy and Business Goals

The ultimate goal of a data strategy is to drive business impact . This means that data projects should be aligned with the company’s objectives and key results (OKRs) and that data should be used to make decisions and actions that have a measurable effect on the business.

To achieve this alignment, data teams should collaborate closely with business stakeholders to understand their needs, challenges, and opportunities. Data teams should also communicate the value proposition of data projects clearly and frequently to the business stakeholders, using metrics and dashboards that show the progress and impact of data initiatives.

Data teams should also adopt an agile and iterative approach to data projects, using techniques such as prototyping, testing, feedback, and experimentation. This allows them to validate their assumptions, learn from failures, and deliver value faster.

Some examples of data projects that can drive business impact are:

  • Customer segmentation and personalization: Using data to group customers based on their behaviors, preferences, needs, and potential value, and providing them with tailored offers, recommendations, and experiences.
  • Customer retention and churn prediction: Using data to identify customers who are at risk of leaving or reducing their engagement with the company and taking proactive actions to retain them or win them back.
  • Product development and innovation: Using data to test new products or features with customers, measure their satisfaction and feedback, and iterate on the design or functionality.
  • Operational efficiency and optimization: Using data to monitor and improve the performance of business processes, such as production, supply chain, logistics, inventory management, quality control, etc.
  • Risk management and compliance: Using data to detect and prevent fraud, anomalies, errors, or violations of regulations or policies.

Conclusion

Data is a powerful asset that can help you achieve your business goals. However, to use it effectively, you need a clear data strategy that aligns with your company objectives. You also need a robust data governance framework that ensures data quality, security, and accessibility. And you need a culture of data-driven decision-making that leverages data to take actions that have a measurable impact on the business.
